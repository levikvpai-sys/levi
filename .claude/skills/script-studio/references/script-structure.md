# The Screenwriter — Structure, Beats & Dialogue

Structure is what makes a script feel *inevitable* instead of random. Pick the
framework by length, then fill it with lines that carry emotion and "crackers."

---

## Pick your structure by length

| Format | Length | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Micro-Beat | 8–60s | Reels/TikTok/Shorts, ads, hooks-driven content |
| Mini-Story | 60–180s | UGC ads, explainers, brand stories |
| Save the Cat (15 beats) | short film → feature | narrative films, trailers |
| 8-Sequence method | feature | complex features, series episodes |

---

## Short-form: the Micro-Beat structure (memorize this)

The workhorse for social video. Five moves:

1. **HOOK (0–3s)** — stop the scroll (see `hooks.md`). Promise or provoke.
2. **CONTEXT / SETUP (3–8s)** — one sentence of "why this matters to you." Just
   enough stakes; no over-explaining.
3. **ESCALATION / VALUE (the body)** — deliver the goods in rising steps. Each
   step earns the next second. Use open loops and re-hooks. This is 60–70% of runtime.
4. **PAYOFF / TURN (near end)** — the reveal, the result, the punchline, the
   transformation. Pays back the hook's promise. This is the "cracker."
5. **CTA / BUTTON (last 1–3s)** — one clear ask ("follow for part 2," "save this")
   or a resonant final image/line that loops back to the hook.

> The **loop trick**: make the last line flow back into the first so the video
> replays seamlessly — replays inflate watch-time and reach.

## Save the Cat! — the 15 beats (long-form / narrative)

Blake Snyder's beat sheet. Percentages are of total runtime/pages.

1. **Opening Image (0–1%)** — a snapshot of the "before" world/tone; the mood-setter.
2. **Theme Stated (~5%)** — someone hints at the lesson the hero must learn.
3. **Set-Up (1–10%)** — establish hero, world, flaws, stakes, what's missing.
4. **Catalyst (~10%)** — the inciting incident; life-changing news/event.
5. **Debate (10–20%)** — hero hesitates: "should I? can I?" The last of the old world.
6. **Break into Two (~20%)** — hero commits and steps into the new world/goal.
7. **B Story (~22%)** — a new relationship (often love/mentor) that carries the theme.
8. **Fun and Games (20–50%)** — "the promise of the premise"; the trailer moments.
9. **Midpoint (~50%)** — a false victory or false defeat; stakes raise, clock starts.
10. **Bad Guys Close In (50–75%)** — external pressure + internal doubt tighten.
11. **All Is Lost (~75%)** — the lowest point; often a "whiff of death."
12. **Dark Night of the Soul (75–80%)** — hero wallows; hopelessness before the idea.
13. **Break into Three (~80%)** — the revelation; hero fuses A+B story lessons, acts.
14. **Finale (80–99%)** — hero storms the castle; proves change; resolves the theme.
15. **Final Image (99–100%)** — mirror of the Opening Image; shows the transformation.

To place beats: `total_scenes × percentage` = the scene it lands in (Midpoint =
scenes × 0.5).

## Hero's Journey (condensed 12 steps)

Ordinary World → Call to Adventure → Refusal → Meeting the Mentor → Crossing the
Threshold → Tests/Allies/Enemies → Approach the Inmost Cave → Ordeal → Reward →
The Road Back → Resurrection → Return with the Elixir. Use for myth/epic tone;
maps roughly onto Save the Cat.

## The 8-Sequence method (feature-length engine)

Break a feature into 8 ~10–15 min sequences, each a mini-movie with its own
tension-and-release: (1) Status quo + inciting incident, (2) Predicament + lock-in,
(3) First obstacle + raised stakes, (4) First culmination / midpoint push,
(5) Subplot + rising action, (6) Main culmination / "all is lost," (7) New tension
from the resolution, (8) Resolution. Great for managing the sagging middle.

---

## Scene structure (any length)

Every scene should have: **a goal, an obstacle, and a turn.** A scene that ends in
the same emotional place it began is dead weight. Enter late, leave early — start
as close to the conflict as possible, cut the moment the point lands.

**Value shift**: a scene should move a charge from + to − or − to + (safe→danger,
hope→despair). If nothing shifts, cut or combine.

## Dialogue & "Crackers"

"Crackers" = the lines and turns people quote, screenshot, and rewatch. Engineer them.

- **Subtext over on-the-nose.** Characters rarely say exactly what they mean; the
  gap is where drama lives.
- **Every character wants something** in every scene — dialogue is strategy toward it.
- **Give each character a distinct voice** — rhythm, vocabulary, verbal tics. You
  should know who's speaking with the names covered.
- **Compress.** Cut every word the meaning survives without. Real speech trimmed of
  filler.
- **The turn/button**: end scenes and beats on a strong line — a twist, a punchline,
  a gut-punch, or an unanswered question.
- **Specificity is charisma.** Concrete nouns and precise detail feel true; generic
  lines feel fake.
- **Rule of three & callback**: set up a phrase/image early, pay it off later — the
  callback lands as a "cracker."
- **Say it once, hard.** Don't repeat the theme five times; state it powerfully once.

## Screenplay formatting (long-form)

```
INT. LOCATION - DAY                    ← scene heading (slugline)

Action described in present tense, lean and visual. Only what
the camera can see or the mic can hear.

                    CHARACTER NAME
          (parenthetical, sparingly)
     The dialogue lives here, centered under the name.

                                        CUT TO:
```

Rules: present tense; ~1 page ≈ 1 minute of screen time; write only what's
seeable/hearable; CAPS a character on first appearance and for key SFX; keep
action paragraphs short (1–4 lines) for readable white space.

## Ad / commercial micro-structures (high-conversion)

- **PAS**: Problem → Agitate → Solution. Fast and reliable for pain-point products.
- **AIDA**: Attention → Interest → Desire → Action.
- **Before–After–Bridge**: show the pain, show the dream, bridge = your product.
- **Feature→Benefit→Meaning**: what it is → what it does → what it *means for them*.
- **Hook–Story–Offer**: stop them, make them feel, then ask.

Match the structure to the goal, then wrap it in the Micro-Beat pacing above.
