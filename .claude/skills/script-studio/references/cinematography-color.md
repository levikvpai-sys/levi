# The Cinematographer / Colorist — Color, Grade & Light

Color is emotion, not decoration. Every palette choice should serve the mood arc
of the script. Grade with intent; light with motivation.

---

## Color psychology (what each hue *does* to a viewer)

| Color | Feels like | Common story use |
|---|---|---|
| **Red** | passion, danger, power, love, rage | desire, threat, violence, alarm |
| **Orange** | warmth, energy, comfort, nostalgia | golden-hour warmth, friendliness, memory |
| **Yellow** | joy, optimism, but also madness/sickness | cheer; or unease/obsession when sour |
| **Green** | nature, growth, envy, decay, unnatural | corruption, sickness, money, the eerie |
| **Blue** | calm, sadness, cold, isolation, tech | melancholy, loneliness, night, the clinical |
| **Teal/Cyan** | modern, cool, dreamlike | sci-fi, cool contrast against warm skin |
| **Purple** | mystery, royalty, fantasy, decadence | magic, luxury, the surreal, power |
| **Pink** | tenderness, innocence, playful, artificial | romance, youth, camp, hyper-real |
| **White** | purity, void, sterility, isolation | innocence; or emptiness/institutional dread |
| **Black** | power, death, elegance, the unknown | threat, mourning, sophistication |
| **Brown/Sepia** | age, earth, rustic, past | period pieces, memory, groundedness |

Warm hues (red/orange/yellow) → energy, intimacy, danger. Cool hues
(blue/green/purple) → distance, calm, melancholy, mystery. The *contrast* between
warm and cool is where cinematic tension lives.

---

## Color schemes (how to build a palette)

- **Complementary** (opposite on the wheel, e.g. teal↔orange) — max contrast &
  tension; makes subjects pop. The dominant blockbuster look.
- **Analogous** (neighbors, e.g. amber→orange→red) — harmony, cohesion, a single
  emotional key. Great for warm nostalgia or cold dread.
- **Monochromatic** (one hue, varied value/saturation) — mood-drenched, stylized,
  focused (e.g. the blue of *sicario*-style night).
- **Triadic** (three evenly spaced) — vibrant, playful, energetic; animation/pop.
- **Split-complementary** — contrast with less harshness than pure complementary.

**Teal-and-orange** works because skin tones sit in the orange range and pushing
shadows/backgrounds teal creates complementary contrast that makes people pop.
Powerful but overused — use deliberately, or subvert it for a signature look.

---

## Palette by mood/genre (starting points)

- **Romance / nostalgia** → warm ambers, soft pinks, golden-hour; low contrast, glow.
- **Thriller / crime** → desaturated teal-and-orange, hard contrast, deep shadows.
- **Horror** → sickly greens, cold blues, crushed blacks; or stark high-key dread.
- **Sci-fi** → cyan/teal + clinical white, or neon magenta+cyan (cyberpunk).
- **Drama** → naturalistic, muted, restrained saturation; let faces carry it.
- **Comedy / lifestyle** → bright, saturated, high-key, warm and inviting.
- **Luxury / brand** → limited palette, deep blacks, one accent color, controlled.
- **Aspirational UGC / ad** → clean, bright, warm skin, punchy but not fake.

## Grading vocabulary (so notes are executable)

- **Lift / Gamma / Gain** = shadows / midtones / highlights. Grade in that order.
- **Color balance**: push shadows one way, highlights the complementary way
  (e.g. teal shadows, warm highlights) for cinematic split-tone.
- **Contrast & S-curve**: crush blacks / roll highlights for filmic depth.
- **Saturation vs vibrance**: vibrance protects skin; global saturation can go garish.
- **LUT**: a preset "look" applied as a base, then hand-tuned. Name the reference
  look you're after (e.g. "Kodak 2383 print emulation," "bleach-bypass," "Teal&Orange").
- **HSL qualifier**: isolate one color (e.g. desaturate everything but the red dress).
- **Film emulation / grain**: adds organic texture; reduces the "digital" feel.

**How to spec color in a script/shotlist:** name the *palette*, the *mood word*, the
*contrast level*, and a *reference*. Example: `COLOR: cold teal shadows + warm skin,
high contrast, crushed blacks — ref: Blade Runner 2049 night interiors.`

## Lighting styles (the other half of "the look")

- **High-key**: bright, low shadow, upbeat/clean — comedy, lifestyle, beauty, ads.
- **Low-key**: deep shadows, high contrast — drama, noir, thriller, mystery.
- **Chiaroscuro**: extreme light/dark; sculpts faces; painterly tension.
- **Motivated lighting**: every light has an in-world source (window, lamp, screen)
  → realism.
- **Practical lights**: visible in-frame sources (neon, candles, lamps) → atmosphere.
- **Golden hour**: warm, low, soft — the easiest "cinematic" win, romance/nostalgia.
- **Hard vs soft**: hard light = drama, edges, tension; soft light = flattering, calm.
- **Color contrast in light**: warm key + cool fill (or vice-versa) = instant depth.

Direction of light: front = flat/safe; side = drama/texture; back = separation/glow
(rim light); top = ominous; under = horror/unnatural.

## The mood arc (color as story)

Map the palette to the emotional journey, don't pick one look for the whole piece:
- Start and end in contrasting palettes to *show* the transformation (cold→warm as
  a character heals; warm→cold as they lose).
- Shift saturation/contrast at turning points (drain color at "all is lost," bloom
  it at the payoff).
- Give the hero/theme a **signature color** and let it recur.

---

## Research protocol (per project)

1. Pull 2–3 reference frames/films whose look matches the target mood.
2. Name *why* they work (palette, contrast, light direction, grade).
3. Translate into an executable spec (palette + mood word + contrast + reference +
   optional hex swatches) the shooter or the AI generator can follow.
